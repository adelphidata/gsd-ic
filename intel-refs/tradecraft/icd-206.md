# ICD 206 — Sourcing Standards

Intelligence Community Directive 206 — *Sourcing Requirements for Disseminated Analytic Products* — establishes
the standards that govern how intelligence sources are described, characterized, and cited in finished analytic
products disseminated across the IC and to IC customers. ICD 206 is the companion directive to ICD 203: while
ICD 203 §C.6.5(1) states the obligation to "properly describe sources and their reliability, credibility, and
quality," and ICD 203 §C.6.5(9) states the obligation to "make accurate use of citations and references,"
ICD 206 elaborates the specific mechanics — what a compliant source description contains, how credibility and
reliability are rated, and how citations are formatted in disseminated products. Compliance with ICD 206 is a
necessary condition for satisfying ICD 203's analytic tradecraft standard on sourcing. Any analytic product
that undergoes `gsd-icd-203-enforcer` audit is evaluated against both directives.

## The Sourcing Discipline

Source description is a distinct analytic discipline, not an administrative afterthought. Every source cited
in a finished analytic product must carry two orthogonal characterizations before the analytic claim it
supports can be weight-assigned by the reader:

**Reliability** addresses the source itself — its track record, access, motivations, and the degree to which
its reporting has proven accurate over time. The IC uses an A–F reliability scale (equivalent to NATO STANAG
2511 categories) running from "completely reliable" (A) to "reliability cannot be judged" (F). Reliability
is a property of the source, not of any individual report, and is established or updated across multiple
reporting cycles.

**Credibility** addresses the specific information in a single report — whether the content is internally
consistent, independently corroborated, and consistent with what is known about the subject from other
collection. The IC uses a 1–6 credibility scale (NATO STANAG 2511 equivalent) running from "confirmed by
other sources" (1) to "truth cannot be judged" (6). Credibility is a property of the individual report or
piece of information, not of the source as a whole.

Together, the reliability rating and credibility rating form a parametric judgment — e.g., "B-2" — that
tells the reader both how much to trust the source historically and how well this particular piece of
information holds up. An analytic claim built on a D-1 source (low reliability, but the specific information
is multiply corroborated) carries a different epistemic weight than a claim built on an A-5 source (excellent
track record, but this specific report is not corroborated). Analysts must communicate that distinction;
ICD 206 requires it.

Source description applies to all collection disciplines: HUMINT, SIGINT, GEOINT, OSINT, MASINT, and
open-source technical reporting. The characterization method is discipline-specific in some particulars
but the reliability/credibility structure is universal.

## Source Description Elements

A compliant ICD 206 source description includes the following elements for each source cited:

- **Source type** — the collection discipline: HUMINT report, SIGINT collection, GEOINT imagery or
  geospatial product, OSINT publication (open web, academic, government), MASINT signal or measurement
  data, or a liaison/partner-government report. Mixed-discipline assessments cite each source type
  separately.
- **Reliability rating** — the A–F characterization for the source (see The Sourcing Discipline above).
  When reliability cannot be established for a new source, the analyst records "F" (reliability cannot
  be judged) rather than omitting the field.
- **Credibility rating** — the 1–6 characterization for the specific information (see The Sourcing
  Discipline above). When corroboration is absent, the analyst records "6" rather than omitting the field.
- **Access** — whether the source's access to the reported information is firsthand (direct observation or
  participation), indirect (hearsay, reporting by others), or unknown. Firsthand access elevates credibility;
  indirect access depresses it. Unknown access requires a more conservative credibility assignment.
- **Collection date** — the date on which the information was collected or the intelligence report was
  dated at the originator. Timeliness degrades credibility for rapidly changing targets; analysts must
  flag information whose collection date predates significant developments.
- **Dissemination chain** — the path from originator to the analytic product: originator (the collecting
  agency or national intelligence element) → any intermediate reporting chains → the analyst producing
  the finished product. For classified sources, the description abstracts above the source's classification:
  a properly described source in an UNCLASSIFIED product does not reveal collection methods or source
  identities, but does convey the reliability, credibility, and access characterizations the reader needs
  to evaluate the claim.

When multiple sources report the same fact, each source receives its own description. Aggregating source
characterizations ("multiple reports indicate") without per-source reliability and credibility ratings is
a citation violation under ICD 206. See Common Violations.

## Citation Conventions

ICD 206 distinguishes two citation functions in finished analytic products:

**Citation** is the source-of-record reference — it tells the reader which source is the basis for a
given analytic claim. Every factual assertion in a finished product must be traceable to at least one
cited source. Citations do not imply endorsement of the source's judgment; they establish provenance.

**Attribution** is the named-originator reference — it identifies the producing organization or agency
when dissemination rules permit. Attribution appears when the product's dissemination authority allows
the reader to know who produced the underlying report. Attribution is not always possible (caveated
or compartmented sources may not permit naming the originator), but citation is always required.

Citation mechanics:

- **Footnote or endnote** — preferred for long-form analytic products (assessments, memoranda, national
  intelligence estimates) that support a structured reference section. Each footnote records the source
  type, reliability rating, credibility rating, and collection date at minimum. Numbered footnotes allow
  the reader to trace any claim back to its source without interrupting the narrative flow.
- **Inline parenthetical** — acceptable for short products (one to three pages) or tactical products
  where footnote apparatus is impractical. Inline format: (Source type; reliability; credibility;
  collection date) immediately following the claim.
- **Cite once per fact, not once per paragraph.** A source supporting multiple claims in a paragraph
  is cited at each claim. A single footnote at the paragraph end implies all claims in the paragraph
  rest on that source — a false precision that violates ICD 206 §5.
- **Distinguish citation from analysis.** Analytic judgments that synthesize across sources are not
  the direct output of any single source; they are labeled as analytic judgments (see ICD 203 §C.6.5(3)
  on distinguishing intelligence from judgment) and cite the underlying sources that inform the judgment.

## Common Violations

ICD 206 identifies categories of non-compliance that recur in finished analytic products. The following
patterns are treated as violations in `gsd-icd-203-enforcer` audits:

**Uncited assertions.** A factual claim in a finished product that carries no citation. Uncited assertions
fail ICD 203 §C.6.5(1) (source description) and §C.6.5(9) (citation accuracy) simultaneously. Every
factual assertion must be cited; analytic judgments must be labeled as such and tied to the sources that
inform them.

**Aggregated source characterization without per-source reliability.** Language like "multiple reports
indicate," "several sources suggest," or "reporting indicates" without citing each source individually
and assigning reliability and credibility ratings to each. Aggregation conceals the epistemic quality
of the evidence. Analysts may note corroboration ("three independent HUMINT sources assess…"), but each
source must still be individually characterized.

**Missing collection-date context.** A cited source whose collection date is absent or ambiguous. For
time-sensitive targets — military movements, leadership positions, program status — a source's collection
date is integral to the claim's validity. Citing a report without a collection date prevents the reader
from assessing whether the information is still current.

**Unattributed analytic judgments.** An analytic judgment presented as though it were a sourced fact —
that is, without the appropriate judgment marker ("Adelphi assesses," "we judge," "our assessment is")
and without a citation to the sources informing the judgment. Judgments that appear to float free of
both sourcing and analytic attribution are treated as a dual violation: a sourcing violation (no citation)
and an analytic-standards violation (failure to distinguish judgment from fact per ICD 203 §C.6.5(3)).

**Reliability/credibility field omission.** A source description that records the source type and
collection date but omits the reliability and/or credibility rating. ICD 206 does not permit partial
source descriptions; both fields are mandatory. When the rating genuinely cannot be established, the
analyst records F (reliability) or 6 (credibility) explicitly rather than leaving the field blank.

**Overstated reliability.** Assigning a reliability rating higher than the source's track record
supports — typically to elevate the apparent strength of an analytic conclusion. ICD 206 §4 prohibits
reliability inflation; the standard is the source's demonstrated historical performance, not the analyst's
confidence in the current report.

## How gsd-icd-203-enforcer Uses This Ref

The `gsd-icd-203-enforcer` agent reads ICD 206 standards when auditing an analytic artifact for
source-attribution discipline. The audit workflow is:

1. The enforcer scans the artifact for every factual assertion and analytic judgment.
2. For each assertion, it checks for a corresponding citation (footnote, endnote, or inline parenthetical).
3. For each citation, it checks that the source description includes source type, reliability rating,
   credibility rating, access characterization, and collection date.
4. Aggregated source language triggers a per-source characterization check; missing individual citations
   for each aggregated source produce a finding.
5. Findings are classified as `sourcing` (missing or incomplete source description) or `citation`
   (missing citation, aggregation without per-source detail, or collection date absent).

All ICD 206 failures surface in the enforcer's output under the `## ICD 203 VIOLATIONS FOUND` block with
the `sourcing` or `citation` category tag. Each finding identifies the paragraph or section of the artifact,
the specific violation type, and the applicable ICD 206 section reference. Violations carry a severity of
**Major** (uncited assertions, reliability/credibility omission) or **Minor** (partial date context, inline
vs. footnote format deviation in a product that supports footnotes).

The enforcer does not apply ICD 206 to:

- **Working notes and draft artifacts** — only finished, dissemination-ready products are in scope.
- **Metadata fields** — frontmatter and structured data fields are not analytic assertions and do not
  require ICD 206 citations.
- **Classification markings** — paragraph-level and document-level classification markings are governed
  by separate classification guides, not by ICD 206.
